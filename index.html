    Meet RAG: an open-book exam for AI

    Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a way of building AI apps where the system first retrieves the most relevant pieces of your documents, then gives only those pieces to the language model so it can generate an answer grounded in them.

    Think of an open-book exam: you don't re-read the whole textbook for each question — you flip to the right page. RAG needs the "pages" to exist first. Document processing and chunking create those pages.

    The RAG pipeline — click a stage, or press play

    📌 Remember

    • RAG retrieves first, then generates: the model answers from the few chunks it is given.
    • Sending a whole document is slow and costly, and it may not even fit the context window.
    • Chunking and embedding happen once, in advance (indexing), not for every question.

    Module 2 · 6 min

    What is text chunking?

    Four words you need, explained in one line each.

    Document

    Any source of knowledge: a PDF, web page, Word file, or slide deck.

    Text extraction

    Pulling plain text out of that file, then cleaning it (removing headers, page numbers, extra spaces).

    Chunk

    One small, self-contained piece of the text — a few sentences or a paragraph.

    Chunking

    The process of dividing the clean text into chunks so each one can be stored and searched on its own.

    “

    A large textbook is like a complete document. Instead of searching through every page every time, we divide it into smaller meaningful sections so the relevant information can be found more easily.

    Why does context matter?

    Chunk without context

    “…and a late fee of Rs 500.”

    A fee for what? Library? Exams? The chunk is too small to be useful on its own.

    Chunk with context

    “Late registration is allowed only with the approval of the faculty advisor and a late fee of Rs 500.”

    Self-contained. Whoever retrieves this chunk understands it.

    Benefits of good chunking

    • Easier retrievalSmall pieces can be matched precisely to a question.
    • More focused contextThe model sees what matters, not 200 pages.
    • Better document managementUpdate or delete one section without reprocessing everything.
    • Less unnecessary contextFewer tokens → faster, cheaper answers.
    • Controlled LLM inputYou decide exactly what the model is allowed to read.
    Key idea: retrieval can only return chunks that exist. If chunking cuts an answer in half, the best search engine in the world can only find half an answer. Chunking quality limits retrieval quality.

    Two broad approaches

    Fixed-size chunking

    every 20 words

    Cut every N words (or characters, or tokens), no matter where sentences end. Simple and predictable.

    Sentence / structure-aware

    ≤ 20 words, whole sentences

    Respect natural boundaries — sentences, paragraphs, headings — and pack them up to a limit.

    Both columns are generated live by PRISM's real chunking engine from the same text.

    📌 Remember

    • A chunk is a small, self-contained piece of a document, and chunking is the act of cutting it.
    • Retrieval can only return chunks that exist, so chunk quality limits answer quality.
    • Fixed-size = predictable but cuts sentences. Structure-aware = keeps meaning, sizes vary.

    Module 3 · 8 min · Core concept

    Chunk size and overlap

    Two dials control almost every chunker. Learn what each one does.

    Dial 1 — Chunk size

    Chunk size decides how much text goes into each chunk.

    Small chunks

    • More focused information
    • Precise matches
    • More segments to store and search
    • May lose surrounding context

    Large chunks

    • More surrounding context
    • Fewer segments
    • May include irrelevant information
    • Uses more of the model's context window

    Try it · Turn the size dial

    Dial 2 — Chunk overlap

    Overlap means repeating a few words from the end of one chunk at the start of the next one — like a bridge between neighbours.

    Chunk 1Retrieval-Augmented Generation retrieves relevant information from external documents.
    ⇢
    Chunk 2External documents provide context that helps the language model generate grounded responses.

    The repeated phrase (in coral) tells a reader of Chunk 2 what "external documents" refers to — continuity is preserved.

    Try it · The boundary problem

    Fixed chunks of 14 words over the attendance rules. Look where the cut lands:

    The trade-offs

    Too little overlap

    Ideas that cross a boundary get split, and each half can lose its meaning.

    Too much overlap

    Lots of repeated text → more storage, more embeddings to compute, and near-duplicate search results.

    No universal answer

    The right size and overlap depend on your documents, your questions, and your model's limits. Teams test and measure.

    What do we count in? Characters, words, tokens, sentences

    Character-based

    Split every N characters. Easy, but can cut words in half.

    "Attend|ance is…"
    Word-based

    Split every N words. Easy for humans to read — what PRISM's simulator uses.

    "Attendance is | recorded…"
    Token-based

    Split every N tokens — the units a model actually reads. A token is often a word or part of a word.

    "un" "believ" "able"
    Sentence-aware

    Keep whole sentences together and pack them up to a size limit.

    "…exam. | Hall tickets…"
    Important technical distinction: LLM context windows and embedding-model input limits are measured in tokens, not words. In English, one token is roughly ¾ of a word on average, but it varies by tokenizer and language. Production chunkers usually measure size in tokens with the model's own tokenizer. PRISM's word-based simulator is a simplified teaching tool, not a production tokenizer.

from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(
    chunk_size=500,     # measured in characters by default
    chunk_overlap=50,
)
chunks = splitter.split_text(handbook_text)
print(len(chunks), chunks[0])

      It tries paragraph breaks first, then lines, then spaces — a structure-aware strategy. Size can be measured in tokens by passing a tokenizer-based length function.

      The core of PRISM's fixed-size chunker (JavaScript)

      const S = C - O;              // step: size minus overlap
for (let start = 0; start < N; start += S) {
  const end = Math.min(start + C, N);
  chunks.push(words.slice(start, end));
  if (end === N) break;     // no empty or duplicate tail
}

      Full, commented source: js/chunking.js. Overlap is clamped to C − 1 so S ≥ 1 and the loop always ends.

      Key takeaways

      1. RAG retrieves before it generates. Models answer from the chunks you give them.
      2. Chunking creates the searchable units. Bad chunks mean bad retrieval, however good the model.
      3. Size trades focus for context. Overlap trades redundancy for continuity.
      4. Measure in tokens in production — models count tokens, not words.
      5. There is no magic number. Match the strategy to the document's structure, then test with real questions.

        Text Chunking for RAG: Cheat Sheet

        One page · keep it next to your keyboard

        The pipeline

        Document → Extract text → Clean → Chunk → Embed → Store (vector DB) → Question → Retrieve top-k → LLM answer

        Everything before the question happens once (indexing). Retrieval can only return chunks that exist, so chunk quality limits answer quality.

        Key terms

        • Chunk: a small, self-contained piece of a document.
        • Chunk size (C): how much text goes in each chunk.
        • Overlap (O): text repeated from the end of one chunk at the start of the next.
        • Step: S = C − O. Each new chunk starts S units later.
        • Token: the unit models read (≈ ¾ of an English word). Limits are counted in tokens.
        • Embedding: numbers that capture meaning, used for semantic search.

        Strategies

        StrategyGood atWatch out for
        Fixed-size (words/chars)Simple, predictableCuts sentences
        Token-basedExact model limitsCan cut mid-word
        Sentence / structure-awareKeeps meaning wholeSizes vary

        Production splitters usually combine them: count tokens, but cut at paragraph or sentence boundaries.

        Choosing settings: checklist

        1. Look at the document's structure (rules, FAQs, sections, tables).
        2. Find your embedding model's token limit and stay well below it.
        3. Start moderate, e.g. a few hundred tokens with 10–20% overlap.
        4. Test with real questions: is the answer retrieved whole?
        5. Adjust, re-test, and keep notes. There is no universal best.

        Symptoms → fixes

        • Answers come back in fragments → larger or sentence-aware chunks, or add overlap.
        • Retrieved text is mostly irrelevant → smaller chunks.
        • Near-duplicate results → reduce overlap.
        • Right meaning, different words missed → embeddings, not keywords.
        • Garbage text (page numbers, broken words) → fix extraction and cleaning first.
